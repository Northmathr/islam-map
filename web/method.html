Sources & method

How the mosque count is put together, and what it cannot tell you

← Back to the map

This map answers three counting questions: how many mosques there are, how that has changed, and what is in planning now. Everything below is what sits behind those three numbers, including the parts that are uncertain.

It covers the whole United Kingdom, which means stitching three censuses together. England and Wales were counted by the ONS in March 2021, Northern Ireland by NISRA in March 2021, and Scotland by NRS in March 2022 — a year later, on its own timetable, with its own categories. Any UK-wide population figure here is therefore a sum across two reference dates, and the panel states which year a district's numbers come from.

There is no official register of mosques

Nobody publishes one. No government body maintains a list of places of worship by faith, so any figure you see — including this one — is assembled from sources that were collected for other reasons. That is the single most important thing to know before reading any mosque count, here or elsewhere.

It also means “mosque” is not a fixed category. A purpose-built mosque with a dome and a minaret is obviously one. A converted terraced house used for daily prayer, a unit on an industrial estate, a university prayer room — different sources draw that line in different places, and that definitional spread is a real part of the gap between published estimates. It is not all missing data.

Three sources, merged

Each source misses something different, so the map takes the union of all three and deduplicates locations within 150 metres of one another.

OpenStreetMap via Overpass

Good at Precise coordinates — a mapped building, not an approximation.

Falls short Volunteer-made and incomplete. Carries no opening dates.

Charity Commission bulk register

Good at Official and citable: every entry has a registered charity number you can look up.

Falls short The address is a contact address. Usually the mosque, sometimes a trustee’s home or an accountant’s office.

Planning applications via PlanIt

Good at Official, dated, and the only source that carries a timeline.

Falls short Only covers premises that needed permission, and an approval is a decision rather than a finished building.

Why not just OpenStreetMap?

Because it holds roughly three quarters of them. OpenStreetMap alone finds 1,314 mosques in England and Wales, well short of the ~1,800 the MuslimsInBritain directory lists. Widening the query — relations, building=mosque, name matching, prayer rooms — adds only 55 elements, so the gap is coverage rather than a bad query. Two independent checks measure it:

How the merge works

Points within 150 metres are treated as the same place. That distance is set by the weakest input: postcode centroids are accurate to roughly 100 metres, so anything tighter would split one mosque into several, and anything much looser would start merging genuinely separate mosques in dense inner-city areas. Where sources disagree on position, the OpenStreetMap coordinate wins, because it is a mapped building rather than a postcode centroid.

How mosque charities are identified

Almost every mosque in England and Wales is a registered charity, but you cannot find them by name alone. Searching the register for “mosque”, “masjid” and similar returns 884 charities and misses hundreds that register as an association or a trust. Widening the pattern overshoots in both directions at once: “Sunninghill Parochial Charities” matches on “sunni”, and the Islamic Medical Association is not a place of worship.

So three signals are combined, and every entry records which one caught it:

EvidenceTestn
Name The name contains mosque, masjid or musalla. Unambiguous. 411
Activity The charity’s own description of its work says it runs a mosque — “Acton’s main mosque providing daily prayers”. Catches the ones named after an association. 173
Inferred An Islamic name, registered under Religious Activities, holding land, and not an umbrella body. Weaker, so it is tiered separately. 409

The activity test deliberately ignores the phrase “place of worship”, which is faith-neutral and matched churches, synagogues and mandirs by the hundred. The land test is worth using because 73% of charities whose name says mosque hold land, against 33% of the register at large.

In England and Wales that gives 1,066 matched charities and 993 distinct geocoded locations, against the Ayaan Institute’s 1,179 for the whole UK — close to consistent, since the Charity Commission covers England and Wales only.

Scotland is covered separately, from OSCR’s Scottish Charity Register. It is a different schema with no classification codes, so the “advancement of religion” charitable purpose stands in for the Religious Activities classification, and there is no equivalent of the land flag — the inferred tier there rests on two signals rather than three and is correspondingly weaker. That adds 80 matched charities and 76 locations, for a UK total of 1,069.

Northern Ireland has no equivalent. CCNI publishes no open bulk download, so mosques there rest on OpenStreetMap and planning alone.

Measured against every premises where Muslims gather to pray, 2,187, this register is 15% short. That gap is the definitional problem in numbers: the missing ones are hired halls, dedicated prayer rooms, chaplaincies and temporary premises — exactly the categories a mapped building, a registered charity and a planning application all fail to capture. This register counts mosques as premises; it does not count every place a congregation prays.

The Ayaan Institute report is the most detailed public study of mosque finances and governance, and its 1,884 UK estimate is close to the MuslimsInBritain masjid count, which it uses as one of its sources alongside Charity Commission records. It is not an independent count so much as a careful assembly of the same underlying directories, which is worth knowing before treating the agreement between them as confirmation. The report frames itself as addressing myths about mosque expansion; its figures are nonetheless the best public benchmark available and are used here as one.

The report also exposed a real defect in this pipeline, since corrected. Ayaan identified 1,179 mosques registered as charities across the UK; matching on the name alone found only 884 in England and Wales. Mosques that register as “Newham North Islamic Association” or “Gloucestershire Islamic Trust” carry no worship word in the name at all. Matching now combines three signals — the name, the charity’s own description of its activities, and its registered classification — described under how mosque charities are identified below. That lifted the charity input from 820 usable locations to 993.

What the year slider actually moves

There is no source that dates most mosques. What the slider moves is documentary evidence: the year a location first appears in a charity register or a planning approval.

Two of the three sources carry dates. A charity registration is not an opening date — congregations often register years after they start meeting — and a planning approval is a decision rather than a finished building. Both are nonetheless real evidence that a mosque was there by then, and together they date 1,093 of the register’s 1,842 locations. OpenStreetMap carries no dates at all.

So the headline count at a past year is today’s locations, minus the ones first recorded since. Locations with no dated record are counted in every year, because nothing is known about when they appeared and dropping them would understate the past far more than keeping them overstates it. Any year before the present is marked est. for that reason.

Read it as a floor rather than a count. It is conservative in a known direction: registration and planning both lag the congregation, so a mosque that opened in 1990 and registered as a charity in 2005 appears here in 2005. Real growth over the period was larger than the curve shows, and the early years are higher than the true figure.

Earlier years are thinner because records are thinner. Planning coverage improves over time, so the rise in applications through the series is partly better record-keeping and not purely more activity. The 2023 figure in particular looks like a backfill artefact rather than a real spike.

Classifying applications

Applications are sorted by what they would do to the count, read from the description text. Ordering alone is not enough: “demolition of existing building and erection of a mosque” is a replacement, and a naive demolition-first rule classified the majority of redevelopments as losses — 123 of 148 apparent demolitions were actually rebuilds.

Of 1,668 applications since 2000: 1,203 approved, 214 refused, 178 withdrawn, 73 awaiting a decision. Net effect of approved decisions: +349.

A second ordering trap sat behind the first. “Erection of a single storey extension to the mosque” matches the build rule as surely as “erection of a mosque” does, so 190 extensions, minarets, new roofs and re-laid car parks were being counted as brand new mosques. An addition to a building that is already a mosque is now recognised as an extension whatever verb the description uses, which moved the net effect of approved decisions from +503 to +349.

Census figures

England and Wales come from the ONS Census — TS030 for 2021 and KS209EW for 2011, both via Nomis, at local authority district level (April 2023 boundaries, 318 areas).

Scotland comes from National Records of Scotland, table UV205 of the 2022 census, by council area. Northern Ireland comes from NISRA’s 2021 census by local government district. NISRA’s headline religion table does not break Muslims out at all — it stops at “other religions”, because the question there is built around the Catholic and Protestant division — so the six-category aggregate is used instead. Neither nation has a 2011 comparison here, so the change figures are England and Wales only.

Both devolved figures reconcile against their published totals: Scotland 5,439,843 residents and 119,878 Muslims (2.20%), Northern Ireland 1,903,177 and 10,871 (0.57%).

Religion is the only voluntary question on the census. Around 6% of people leave it blank, and non-response varies by area, so the panel states the local blank rate alongside every figure.

The 2011 table is only published on pre-April-2015 boundaries, so it is summed onto 2023 successors before any change is computed — 41 abolished codes are reconciled, and the resulting 2011 population matches the published figure for England and Wales exactly. Areas are always joined on GSS code, never on name: sources disagree on “Bristol, City of” versus “Bristol”, and a name join drops them silently.

Census disclosure control perturbs individual cells, so reconciliation checks assert a small tolerance rather than exact equality — the 318 districts sum to a 2021 population of 59,597,567 against a published 59,597,540.

Mosques and applications are placed into districts by point-in-polygon on coordinates, never by the name a source supplies: PlanIt’s area name is the planning authority, which is not always the district.

Known limitations

  • Charity contact addresses are the weakest input. A charity-only location is a lead rather than a confirmed building — which is why single-source points are drawn faded.
  • Planning coverage is uneven across authorities and improves over time, so the series understates earlier years.
  • Approval is not completion. Some approved schemes are never built; some are built years later.
  • Northern Ireland has no charity source. CCNI publishes no open bulk download, so its mosques rest on OpenStreetMap and planning alone. MuslimsInBritain list five in the province, so the exposure is small, but it is not equivalent coverage.
  • Planning is thinner outside England. PlanIt aggregates local authority registers unevenly: 1,565 of the applications here are English, 65 Scottish, 37 Welsh and one Northern Irish.
  • Three censuses, two dates. Scotland was counted in 2022 and the rest of the UK in 2021, and Northern Ireland does not publish a Muslim figure in its headline religion table at all — the six-category aggregate is used instead.
  • No prayer-room census. Small musallas inside other buildings are inconsistently captured by every source used here.

Getting a better number

The one thing that would turn the slider into a real history of buildings is a source of establishment dates. Muslims in Britain has historically tracked founding years; using it would be a licensing conversation rather than a scrape. Until then, change over time here means planning decisions, and the map says so.
